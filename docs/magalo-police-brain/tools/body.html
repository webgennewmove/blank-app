Police Brain

MAGALO Constabulary

A blueprint for MAGALO's AI police: officers who observe, report, reason about where you went, search for you, and sometimes get it wrong. Nothing about you is known unless someone saw it, heard it or was told it.

Design blueprint v1 26 October 2026 Status: for review No code · no project files
How to read this

This is a design document for later implementation, written without access to MAGALO's code. Existing crime, heat, XP, arrest and confiscation systems are treated as black boxes behind adapters. It builds on the Citizen Brain v1 contracts: witnesses call 999 (6 s, interruptible), shout, or give statements, and civilian reports carry a last-seen spot and heading but never the player's identity.

Every number here is a playtest hypothesis, not an established optimum. Where a number depends on the player's sprint speed, it's written relative to it (Sp), with 24 studs/s as the worked example.

Design philosophy and gameplay objectives

The goal is police that appear to reason about what the player did. After a chase, a player should be able to explain every police move: "someone rang 999 and said I ran east, so they came from the east; I lost them at the corner, so they searched the alley I'd have taken."

Objectives

  • Intelligent but outsmartable. Officers coordinate, predict obvious routes and search sensibly, but they overrun corners, pick the wrong branch, check the wrong bin and run out of breath.
  • Proportionate. A phone snatch gets one officer who investigates, not a manhunt. Escalation has to be earned by repeat crimes, long evasion and evidence.
  • Escapes are real. A getaway is the outcome of an actual pursuit or search that failed, never a timer that happens to expire.
  • Readable. Radio chatter, a last-known-position marker and officer body language tell the player what the police believe.
  • MAGALO's own identity. On foot first, on British streets, driven by witnesses and investigation, with no weapons.

Rules of fair play

  1. Police act only on what they perceived or were told: sight, sound, reports and radio.
  2. Every piece of information has a source, a time and a confidence, and it decays.
  3. Reinforcements arrive from somewhere you could watch them come from, such as the station or a road at the edge of the map. They never spawn in view or close by.
  4. No officer outruns a sprinting player, and officers tire sooner.
  5. An arrest needs proximity and time, and every arrest attempt is telegraphed and has a window to break free.
  6. Hidden means hidden: no tracking through walls, and hiding spots are opened only for a reason.
  7. Every search ends.
  8. Mistakes are allowed, and visible.

Recommended police brain architecture

Three layers, one shared picture. A central Dispatch owns cases and decides who responds. Each active case gets a lightweight squad that owns the shared tactical picture: where the suspect was last seen, how confident everyone is, who does what, and where to search. Each officer runs a small state machine with local utility choices, its own senses and its own movement. Diagram A shows the whole structure and B the officer's states.

This is the simplest combination that delivers coordination without omniscience. A state machine gives predictable, debuggable officer behavior. Utility appears only where officers choose among options (which corner, which bin, which junction). A per-case blackboard is the single place shared knowledge lives, which makes "what do the police know?" one inspectable object. Section 15 compares this with three alternatives.

  • Local utility, kept small. In Search, an officer scores candidate nodes and hiding spots by probability per second of travel. In Pursue, it chooses direct chase or graph route, sprint or pace (stamina), and when to reach. In Patrol, it picks the next beat point. Each choice uses 2–3 factors.
  • Two blackboards. The officer blackboard holds what this officer has seen or heard, plus its role, target and stamina. The case blackboard holds the shared belief: knowledge state, last known position (LKP) and heading, the occupancy map, cleared nodes, opened hiding spots, roles and escalation. Officers write to the case only by radio, which has delay.
  • No identical officers. Each officer gets small spawn-time variations: fitness (±20% sprint duration), diligence (time spent per check), reaction jitter (±0.1 s) and seeded tie-breaking. Roles differ too, so two officers rarely make the same move.

Duplicate reports about the same suspect merge into one case and raise confidence slightly. Reports seed the same occupancy belief that sightings do, fast-forwarded by their age, so an old report naturally means a wide, vague search.

Choosing who responds

priority = severity × evidence × recency × (1 + 0.5 × heat tier) × repeat multiplier unit score = ETA (graph path ÷ jog speed) + preemption penalty + beat-departure cost − approach-angle bonus choose : lowest score among units in Patrol or Return, or on lower-priority work backup : prefer units whose approach is more than 60° from existing responders
  • Levels set head-count: L1 one unit, L2 up to two, L3 three, L4 four (section 6).
  • Preemption: an L1 investigation can lose its unit to a pursuit elsewhere. An active pursuit can't be stripped unless it's deep in a low-confidence search.
  • Minimum service and caps: every case in Pursuit gets at least one unit if any exists; no case gets more than its level allows. One player's spree can't drain every officer from the server.
  • No units free: the case is logged at L0 and closes as Unsolved after 45 s. Busy servers make low-level theft easier, which is realistic and self-balancing.
  • Officer pool: 2 on-foot officers plus 1 per 2 active players, capped at 8 until profiling says otherwise.

Fair reinforcement spawning

  • Only at the police station and authored arrival points at the edge of the map.
  • At least 60 studs from every active suspect's true position and LKP, and outside every player's line of sight, checked at spawn time.
  • Reinforcements must travel. Dispatch radios "two more units en route", so the player hears them coming.
  • Patrol officers who were already visibly in the world can respond from wherever they are. That's luck, not cheating.

Perception and shared-knowledge model

Officer senses

  • Vision: 60 studs, 120° field of view, plus a 6-stud bubble of 360° awareness at half strength. A target counts as visible if a ray from the officer's eyes reaches its head or upper torso. Vision rays ignore characters and hit walls, fences and props flagged as sight blockers.
  • Reaction: 0.45 s from confirmation to acting, with ±0.1 s per officer.
  • Hearing gives a fuzzy position (±4 studs), never an identity: sprint footsteps 18 studs, landing from a jump 12, bin lid 14, door 16, a civilian's shout 35, a scream 45. Walking and crouching are silent.
  • Reports and radio deliver second-hand knowledge with delay and a quality rating.

Identification: telling a suspect from everyone else

Police must not treat every nearby player as a suspect. An officer can only identify a player who is the suspect of a case it has been briefed on. Identification then builds up while the player is visible:

ID rate per second = 1.6 × description quality × 1 / (1 + (d / 25)²) × behavior × on-scene description quality : officer saw it 1.0 (confirmed instantly) · victim 0.7 · witness saw 0.6 · heard only 0.1 behavior : fleeing from police 2.0 · sprinting 1.4 · walking 0.8 · standing 0.6 on-scene : 1.5 within the report's area, otherwise 1.0 confirmed : ID reaches 1.0

Staying calm and keeping your distance buys real time, and running from police gives you away. The player sees an officer's attention build: a head turn, then a "?" over the officer, then "!" on confirmation. Innocent players are never identified, because they aren't the suspect of any case. At most an officer glances at them.

What the police know: five states

  1. Confirmed

    At least one officer sees and has identified the suspect right now. Position is exact because it is being perceived. Officers pursue.

  2. Occluded

    Seen within the last 2.5 s. Officers run to where the suspect should be (the LKP plus up to 1.5 s of travel along the graph in the last heading) and peek.

  3. Last seen

    An officer saw them, but more than 2.5 s ago. The LKP seeds a probability spread over the street graph, and officers search it.

  4. Reported

    No officer has seen them in this case. A report's spot and heading seed a wider, vaguer spread, aged by how old the report is. Officers respond and investigate.

  5. Lost

    The search has run out of probability or time. The case closes as Evaded (or Unsolved, if police never saw them).

Shared knowledge without omniscience

  • One belief per case. The case blackboard holds the knowledge state, LKP, heading and occupancy map. It changes only through sightings, sounds, reports and "clear" messages.
  • Radio has latency: 0.75–1.5 s. Teammates act on slightly old information, so a sharp turn right after being seen is a real skill.
  • Briefing scope: squad members are briefed on their case. From L3, nearby patrols get a lookout broadcast that lets them identify the suspect, but they stay on their beat unless assigned.
  • The no-omniscience boundary. Officer brains and the search planner never read a player's true position. Only the Perception module touches characters, and only through range, field-of-view and line-of-sight checks. A unit test enforces the dependency, and the debug log tags every officer move with the information source that justified it.
  • Transparency for the player. The wanted player hears dispatch summaries of their own case ("suspect last seen heading east on Kings Road"), sees an LKP marker where police last saw them, and sees the search phase. The full probability map stays hidden.

Pursuit and search behavior specification

On-foot pursuit

In a straight race the player wins. Police catch suspects through coordination, dead ends, obstacles, crowds and player mistakes. That's the intended source of challenge.

  • Steering: while a shapecast between officer and suspect is clear, the officer chases directly, aiming at the suspect's position plus up to 0.4 s of their velocity. Otherwise it follows the graph to the suspect's node. It re-plans at most every 0.5 s, or when the suspect changes node.
  • Corners: when the suspect disappears, the officer runs to the predicted point and peeks for 0.4 s before deciding they're gone.
  • Spacing: a second officer keeps at least 6 studs lateral offset or takes a parallel street. Officers never stack in single file.
  • Support: the pursuer radios a position every 2 s, and asks for backup if the chase passes 10 s or the gap grows for 4 s straight.
  • Cutoffs (L3+): the squad predicts the suspect's next junctions up to 3 hops ahead, weighted by heading and by distance from other officers. A cutoff officer goes to a junction only if it can arrive within 1 s of the suspect's estimated arrival (from the suspect's observed speed). It then holds and watches.
  • Limited map knowledge: some edges are tagged as shortcuts: fence gaps, through-passages, roof routes. Police neither path through nor predict along them, unless an officer saw the suspect use one during this case. Then it becomes known for that case only. Map knowledge pays.
  • Arrest attempts follow section 7: 3.5-stud range, a telegraphed reach, a 1.2 s grab with a chance to break free.

Search: occupancy over the street graph

The search runs on PoliceNet: the Citizen Brain's pedestrian graph plus alley interiors, junction peek points and hiding-spot nodes (bins, deep doorways, behind parked vans), about 150–400 nodes for today's map. Each case in Search keeps an occupancy map: a probability for every node that the suspect is there.

every 0.5 s: 1 spread move most mass about one node per tick (running pace, ~12-stud spacing) weights: away from the LKP 3.0 · sideways 1.0 · back toward it 0.3 · into a hiding spot 0.6 heading bias ×2 for the first 6 s · hiding spots release only 2% per tick 2 clear zero every node any officer can see now (precomputed node visibility); hiding spots excepted 3 evidence noise heard: ×3 on nodes within 6 studs · new sighting or report: reseed 4 normalize total mass back to 1 5 assign each officer takes the unclaimed cluster with the best p per second of travel

Hot0–15 sRun to the nearest clusters, fast corner peeks

  • Hiding spots are opened when their probability is at least 0.20, or when there's evidence from the last 10 s (a lid heard, a sighting nearby) and it's at least 0.10. Each officer may open at most 2 per search. Opening takes 2 s and is telegraphed. If the player is inside, they get a 0.8 s burst-out window while the officer staggers for 0.6 s.
  • Split and regroup: when two clusters each hold 25% or more and there are two officers, they split. When a cluster is cleared, its officer joins the next.
  • Communication: "Checking the north side", "Clear", "Heard something by the bins", "Lost visual at Cobble Row". Each line changes the shared belief, and nearby players hear it.

How a search ends, and how that becomes a getaway

Highest level reachedT-minT-max
L1–L230 s60 s
L345 s90 s

The case closes as Evaded when no officer has confirmed the suspect for at least T-min and no cluster within 150 studs of the LKP holds more than 0.15, or when T-max is reached. Probability beyond 150 studs counts as "left the area". The timings use the highest level the case reached, so de-escalation can't shorten them. A report-only case (police never saw the suspect) closes as Unsolved 45 s after the first officer reaches the scene. Dispatch emits one outcome event, and the XP system awards a getaway only for Evaded (section 7).

De-escalation: during a search, the level drops one step per 30 s without a sighting (never below L1 while the case is open), and extra units peel off visibly. Escalation adds coordination, not superpowers.

Difficulty and tunables

Higher difficulty means better coordination, faster but human reactions, smarter search choices and better use of backup. It never means seeing through walls, spawning in view, outrunning a sprinting player, guaranteed arrests or endless searches. MAGALO can start new players on Gentle and move to Standard after a few getaways, echoing CrimeLogic's reported "easier for new players" change.

Arrest and getaway integration

Arrest

  • Conditions, all checked on the server: the officer is briefed on an open case for this player, has line of sight and has confirmed them; distance ≤ 3.5 studs; the player isn't immune or already being arrested; the officer isn't staggered.
  • Sequence: a 0.25 s telegraphed reach, then a grab lasting 1.2 s during which the player moves at 60%. If they get beyond 4.5 studs, the grab breaks. Otherwise comes a 0.8 s restrain (player locked, cuffing animation), then processing.
  • Escape opportunity: breaking free depends on movement and position, not button-mashing, so it works on mobile. Open ground and obstacles help; corners and dead ends don't.
  • No stunlocks: after a failed grab the officer staggers for 0.6 s and can't grab again for 2 s. The player is immune to every officer for 1.5 s.
  • Cancellation: distance past 4.5 studs, line of sight lost, officer staggered (burst-out), player disconnects, or the case resolves first.
  • One attempt per player: each attempt gets an arrest token, and completion is idempotent.
  • Consequences through adapters: confiscate phones stolen in this case (or per your existing rules), apply existing fines and cooldowns, fail the active mission, no getaway XP.
  • Understandable: the arrest screen shows the case timeline. For example: "Reported by a witness on Market Lane · seen by PC Hale on Kings Road · lost at Cobble Row · found by the bins · caught at Hollow Street."

Getaway

CaseResolved { caseId, userId, outcome, maxLevel, pursuitSeconds, searchSeconds, sightings, reports, closedAt } outcome : Evaded police confirmed the suspect at least once, then lost them → getaway XP eligible Unsolved police never confirmed the suspect → no getaway XP Arrested arrest completed → existing penalties Abandoned suspect disconnected during the case → no rewards

The reward ledger keys on caseId, so an outcome can't pay twice, whatever happens with doors, rejoins or lag. The XP system decides amounts and can scale them by maxLevel and pursuit time.

Multiplayer design

  • One case per suspect. Cases never share heat, arrest state, inventory or XP.
  • Clusters for efficiency: thefts within 15 s and 25 studs of each other are linked into a cluster. Dispatch can cover both scenes with nearby units and the squad coordinates across both, but each case keeps its own knowledge, occupancy map and outcome.
  • One primary case per officer. An officer may assist another case only if its own suspect hasn't been confirmed for 3 s and it confirms the other suspect within 20 studs while that case has fewer than its minimum pursuers. Assists are logged. After switching, an officer commits for at least 3 s.
  • Innocents are safe: identification only works on a case's suspect. An innocent player in a search area may get a short look, which is a free decoy for a friend.
  • Body-blocking: an officer blocked by any player for 2 s says "Move aside, please!" and re-routes. Collisions stay on.

Vehicles plug into the same Dispatch, Case and knowledge model as a new unit type. The on-foot system never depends on them.

Vehicle controllerA real physics chassis driven by the server: pure-pursuit steering to a lookahead point that grows with speed, a target-speed profile that slows for curves, forward shapecasts for obstacles, and a reverse-and-replan stuck recovery.
Road occupancyThe same search model on RoadNet, at vehicle speeds.
  • Detecting a suspect entering a vehicle is a perception event. It upgrades the case and asks Dispatch for car units.
  • Traffic: civilian traffic (if any) yields to sirens. Police avoid ramming civilians and slow at blind junctions.
  • PIT maneuvers only at the highest vehicle tier: speed within a band, a straight segment, nobody else within 30 studs, the police front bumper at the suspect's rear quarter, a 20 s cooldown, and backing off on failure.
  • Abandoned vehicle: the car becomes the LKP, officers dismount, and the on-foot search takes over, seeded at the car.
  • Performance: at most 2–4 active police cars, server network ownership (exploit-safe, at the cost of slight smoothing), simplified collision, despawn when far.

Scenario 2 · Intelligent search

Tanner's Yard is an alley with a north exit and an east gap, and three bins: B1 by the mouth, B2 in the middle, B3 at the far end. The player is Confirmed by Hale with Okoro arriving as backup (L2). The numbers come from the simulation in section 5.

  1. 0.0

    The player sprints round the corner into the mouth of the alley. Hale, 16 studs behind, loses line of sight. LKP: the alley mouth, heading north.

  2. 2.0

    The player climbs into B2, 14 studs in. The lid is audible within 14 studs; Hale is 18 studs away and doesn't hear it.

  3. 2.4

    Hale peeks into the alley: open ground in view, bins concealed, nobody visible. At 2.5 the case moves to Last seen.

  4. 3.5

    Radio delay ends, and the squad assigns roles.

    DecisionsHale takes the north cluster (best probability per second of travel). Okoro takes the east gap (next best, unclaimed). The bins stay shut: all under 0.20 and no evidence.

  5. 6–11

    Each officer clears their exit. 78% of the probability now lies beyond the exits, and the bins fall to 0.10 / 0.06 / 0.05.

    Radio"North side clear." "East gap clear."

  6. 11–30

    Warm phase: both sweep the streets beyond. Okoro walks back through the yard at 18 s. The bins are now 0.07 / 0.04 / 0.03 and not worth opening.

  7. 32.5

    30 s unseen, no local cluster above 0.15: Evaded. At 40 s the player climbs out and can go home.

Variants. If Hale had been within 14 studs when the lid closed, the evidence would raise B2 to 0.16 against B1's 0.09. That meets "evidence and p ≥ 0.10", so Okoro opens B2 at about 6 s. The player gets the 0.8 s burst-out window and the chase resumes. In a dead-end yard the bins collect everything (0.40 / 0.34 / 0.26 by 9 s), so officers open them in order and the hider is likely caught.

Fair outcomes

  • Hiding works after breaking line of sight, hiding quietly, near unseen exits.
  • Noise and dead ends are punished, for reasons the player can see.
  • An opened bin always gives a chance to break out.

AI failure cases

  • The same bin opened twice: the case keeps an opened-spots set.

Debug signals

  • Occupancy heatmap per tick (tester-only node spheres).
  • Inspection log with probability, evidence and budget.
  • Noise events with who could hear them; claim assignments.

Roblox technical architecture and performance

  • Server authority: AI decisions, identification, case state, arrests and gate checks all run on the server. Clients render.
  • Clients: officer animation on a client-created Animator (as Roblox recommends for NPCs), radio audio from bark ids, HUD state from Player attributes (CrimeStatus, ArrestProgress, LKP marker).
  • Scheduling: one Heartbeat loop shared with the Citizen Brain, with separate budgets: police 0.8 ms per frame, civilians 0.6 ms. Officers think at their state's rate (0.5–10 Hz) with random phase. Steering updates run at 10 Hz in Pursue.
  • Event-based perception: noise, reports and radio arrive as events. Vision scans run at 5 Hz in Pursue and Search, 2 Hz otherwise.
  • Spatial queries: candidates come from a distance check against the player list, not part queries. Up to 2 rays per candidate (head, torso), with line-of-sight results cached for 0.2 s while chasing. Use the newer ExcludeInstances / IncludeInstances filters on RaycastParams, which Roblox's physics team describes as much faster for large filter lists.
  • Occupancy at low cost: node-to-node visibility is precomputed once at server start (around 12,000 rays for 400 nodes, spread at 200 per frame, about one second). Clearing is then a lookup filtered by field of view, with no rays. Each tick touches only graph edges. Cap concurrent searches at 4.
  • Navigation: PoliceNet A* in Luau handles almost everything. PathfindingService covers rare off-graph moves through a queue (at most 2 in flight, never in a panic). Pathfinding limits (3,000 studs straight line, failure past 20,000 nodes) don't apply to the graph.
  • Failure recovery: no progress for 1.5 s → sidestep → re-plan with the edge penalized → alternate route → PathfindingService → give up the role and tell the squad. Teleport only if no player can see.
  • Network ownership: server-owned officers (SetNetworkOwner(nil)), so exploiters can't fling or teleport them.
  • RemoteEvent security: no client remote can change police state. The door request is validated server-side (distance, cooldown, gate). Break-free is movement-based, so no "struggle" remote exists to spoof. Bark and HUD remotes are server-to-client only.
  • Memory: a cleanup bag per officer and per case. Case resolution frees occupancy arrays, notes and claims. Logs are ring buffers.
  • Debug instrumentation (tester-gated): an officer overlay showing state, role, target node and a reason string; a case inspector with knowledge state, LKP, an occupancy heatmap, cleared nodes and inspections; a dispatch log with scores; a spawn audit; metrics (escape rate, median chase time, arrests per hour, stuck events, false identifications, which must be 0); and seeded replays.

Critical design review

Four architectures compared

ArchitectureStrengthsWeaknessesVerdict
A. Dispatch + case squads + officer state machines with local utility (recommended)Coordinated but individually fallible officers; one inspectable belief per case; cheap; each layer testable aloneThree layers to build; the squad's role logic needs tuningBest balance of observable intelligence, fairness and cost
B. Independent officers with behavior treesSimple to start; no central stateNo shared belief, so officers clump, duplicate searches and contradict each other; coordination turns into ad-hoc messagingLooks dumb in exactly the moments that matter
C. Central tactical director puppeteering every officerStrong coordination; easy global optimizationFeels robotic and perfect, makes few mistakes, spikes CPU, and is one step from omniscienceToo good to be fair
D. GOAP officers with a blackboardFlexible emergent plansPlanning cost, opaque debugging; police plans are short and knownOverkill

P1 starts with the branch-list search and upgrades to occupancy in P2, so the first playable version isn't blocked on the hardest part.

Overengineering and unnecessary complexity

  • Occupancy, roles and escalation all at once: phased across P1–P3.
  • Rich officer personalities: three small variations are enough.
  • Vehicle AI before foot police are fun: deferred to P6.
  • A city-wide BOLO network: only from L3, only nearby patrols.

Unfair behavior and apparent cheating

  • Spawns behind the player: station and edge-of-map only, with an audit.
  • Perfect cutoffs: ETA validation, limited look-ahead, no shortcut knowledge.
  • Instant identification: description quality and behavior gate it.
  • Seeing through fences or foliage: sight-blocker flags.
  • Psychic responses: processing and radio delays.
  • Unavoidable arrests: telegraph, break-free window, immunity.

Officers getting stuck

  • Doors, props and crowds: authored graph, the recovery ladder, and civilians stepping aside for running officers (Citizen Brain).
  • Unreachable roofs: avoid them in level design or link them with PathfindingLinks; otherwise officers hold the exits.

Performance bottlenecks

  • Humanoid physics and replication first, then raycasts, occupancy and pathfinding. Each has a budget in section 13.

Multiplayer race conditions

  • Door against arrest: exclusive states with no yields between check and commit.
  • Two officers grabbing one player: one arrest token.
  • Evaded and re-sighted in the same frame: Evaded is final; a new crime opens a new case.
  • Outcomes processed twice: ledger keyed by case id.

Excessive response to minor thefts

  • Default L1, one unit.
  • L3 and L4 need repeats, long evasion or a spree.
  • Per-case and per-server caps.

Ways players might exploit it

  • Looping a block forever: cutoffs from L3, holds at L4, and no reward until Evaded.
  • Hiding in view: an officer who sees the bin entered knows where you are.
  • Door camping: the gate.
  • Disconnecting to dodge: Abandoned plus the optional confiscation flag.
  • Innocent friends as decoys or blockers: allowed, it's fair co-op play.
  • Radio-latency dodges: intended skill.

Better deferred

  • Helicopters, police dogs (scent tracking would break fair hiding), drones and SWAT.
  • A CCTV network: very British and a strong future idea if cameras are visible and feed delayed sightings.
  • Recognizing players after a case closes (a "be on the lookout" memory): only if you want it, and as a visible, decaying system.

Recommendation for the first playable version

Build P1, then P2. P1 gives one or two officers on foot who respond to witness reports with believable delay, notice and identify you by sight, chase with corner peeks, search the obvious branches when they lose you, arrest you fairly or lose you for real, and keep your apartment shut until it's over. P2 adds the occupancy search and hiding spots, which is where MAGALO's police become distinctly its own.

P1 playtest checklist

  • Can testers explain why the officer came from where they did?
  • Did anyone feel cheated? Every "cheat" report is a bug or a missing cue.
  • Escape rate between 30% and 70% at L1–L2?
  • Zero arrests of innocent players, zero double rewards.
  • Did the radio and LKP marker help players read the police?

Decisions for you

  1. Approve the three-layer architecture and phasing.
  2. Getaway XP only for Evaded (police saw you), not for Unsolved?
  3. Should an L0 case (no unit came) block apartment entry? Recommended: no.
  4. Confiscate an abandoned case's phones on the next join?
  5. Show the LKP marker and dispatch summaries to the wanted player? Recommended: yes.
  6. Player sprint speed, so Sp-relative numbers can be fixed.
